The project in one slide

An autonomous, protocol-compliant WSPR beacon: an iCEBreaker FPGA board (Lattice iCE40UP5K) plus a PmodGPS module. The FPGA reads UTC from GPS, calibrates its 12 MHz oscillator against the GPS 1PPS, and radiates a continuous-phase 4-FSK carrier from a single GPIO pin — entirely in synthesized logic. Why this design is strict “A signal that looks like WSPR” is a failure. Success is a standard decoder (wsprd, WSJT-X 2.7.0) decoding a real transmission and a spot on wsprnet.org (plan §26, §38). Every deviation from the protocol is a bug by definition.

Phase: the third coordinate

A sinusoid is fully described by three numbers. Watch all three move:

x(t) = A · sin(2π f t + φ)
Why phase continuity is a contract A phase jump at a symbol boundary is, spectrally, a click — it splatters energy over neighbouring tones exactly where the decoder is listening. This is why our RTL has ONE accumulator for the whole 110.6 s transmission and symbol changes only swap the increment (wspr_modulator.vhd). The protocol form is continuous-phase modulation; the discipline is enforced by hardware.

Sampling: the theorem that sets our ceiling

A continuous signal sampled every T = 1/fs seconds keeps its identity only if

fsignal < fs/2   (the Nyquist frequency)

— the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem. Our GPIO pin is sampled by the 12 MHz clock, so nothing above 6 MHz can be represented directly. That single sentence determines our first test bands (part 7).

Ghost images, three costumes (1) Aliasing — the sampled ghost above. (2) The image frequency of a superheterodyne receiver — a station at 2·f_IF away from the wanted one lands exactly on it. (3) Analog-TV ghosts — multipath echoes, the time-domain cousin of spectral images. Our 1-bit DAC manufactures costume (1) deliberately (part 2) and the RF filter must reject it (part 7).

Part 1 · Signals & sampling

The pin is a 1-bit DAC: the zero-order hold

Our “transmitter output stage” is a logic pin: 0 V or 3.3 V, updated once per clock. In DSP language that is a 1-bit zero-order-hold (ZOH) DAC (ZOH, quantization):

  • The staircase changes at most every 83.3 ns (1/12 MHz) — any content above 6 MHz can only exist as an image.
  • Each image at m·fs ± f is weighted by the ZOH envelope w(f) = |sinc(f/fs)| (sinc function).
  • A 1-bit quantizer also spreads broadband quantization noise across the whole spectrum — the band-pass filter, not the DAC, sets the transmitted noise floor.
Consequence The pin cannot make a clean 10 MHz sine. It makes a staircase whose spectrum contains a 10 MHz image — and it is the filter's job to pick that image out. This is not a defect to fix in RTL; it is the architecture (plan §18).
Book Lyons, Understanding Digital Signal Processing, ch. 2 — sampling and aliasing with the best pictures in print. Smith's free Guide to DSP, ch. 3 covers the same ground with less math.
Part 1 · Signals & sampling

Quantization: the noise you cannot remove

Representing a waveform with a finite word (here: one bit) adds an irreducible error — quantization noise:

  • An N-bit DAC has ≈ 6.02·N + 1.76 dB of signal-to-quantization-noise ratio. One bit ⇒ ~7.8 dB — terrible, if you wanted a clean waveform.
  • But the noise is broadband: it spreads over the whole spectrum. A receiver looking at 6 Hz of bandwidth gets a processing advantage of 10·log₁₀(BW) over the spread noise — the same maths as part 3's link budget.
  • This is precisely the logic of delta-sigma converters: coarse quantization + shaping + narrowband filtering. Our beacon is a degenerate, beautiful case: 1 bit, no shaping, and the RF filter does the narrowing.

So why is 1 bit enough here?

  • We transmit one tone at a time — no multi-tone sum needing amplitude accuracy.
  • The decoder needs frequency, not amplitude: non-coherent 4-FSK detection (part 3).
  • The information is spread over 110.6 s — quantization noise integrates out.
  • The pin already exists; a DAC costs money and pins (plan §18: the GPIO is the carrier).
What 1 bit costs you Broadband spurs at every m·fs ± f (part 2), a strong clock line, and harmonics of the square wave. All of it is the filter's job (part 7) — none of it is fixable in RTL, and none of it blocks a decode.

Part 2 · Direct digital synthesis

The NCO: one adder is a radio

A numerically controlled oscillator is the smallest radio brain there is: a register of N bits that adds a constant I every clock, wrapping around modulo 2ᴺ, with its most significant bit wired to the pin.

fout = I · fclk / 2ᴺ   with N = 40:  fout = I · 12 MHz / 2⁴⁰,   one LSB = 1.0914 × 10⁻⁵ Hz
The wrap IS the modulus In numeric_std, phase_acc + unsigned(increment) produces a result as wide as the widest operand; assigning it back to the 40-bit register truncates the carry. That truncation is the modulo — there is no magic (nco.vhd, student-review note). The register is cleared by reset: RF off, plan §31.

Part 2 · Direct digital synthesis

Jitter, spurs, and other honest ugliness

  • Edge jitter: with an arbitrary increment, high/low intervals alternate between ⌊P/2⌋ and ⌈P/2⌉ clocks — every edge carries up to ±1 clock of jitter. The frequency law stays exact in the average, and the receiver integrates thousands of cycles per symbol.
  • Spurs: the phase-accumulator truncation creates discrete spurious lines (DDS spurs, the classic 2ᴺ/gcd family — DDS literature). Narrowband filtering + the decoder's own processing handles them here.
  • Resolution vs spacing: one LSB is 1.09 × 10⁻⁵ Hz; the tone grid needs 0.7324 Hz steps — a margin of ≈134 000 LSBs. (An earlier draft of the header said “hundreds of thousands” — the fresh-student review fixed the arithmetic. Truth wins.)
War story: the unused constant nco.vhd once declared NOMINAL_CLOCK_HZ := 12_000_000 and then never used it — while its own comment explained that no clock constant was needed. A reviewer given only this file reported: “my first reaction was that I must be missing something.” Dead constants read as hidden requirements. Deleted; the provenance moved to a comment.

Part 3 · Communication theory

FSK: information in the slope

Frequency-shift keying maps symbols to frequencies. WSPR is 4-FSK: symbols 0..3 → four tones, spacing Δf = 1.46484375 Hz, symbol rate Rs = 1.46484375 baud. Therefore

modulation index  h = Δf / Rs = 1.0  (exactly)

— the tones are spaced by exactly one symbol rate. At h = 1 the four FSK tones are (nearly) orthogonal over one symbol: a receiver can measure which tone was sent without caring about its exact phase — non-coherent demodulation, which is what wsprd does (wsprd.c sync + symbol integration).

Continuous phase — the WSPR trick

Continuous-phase modulation forbids phase jumps at symbol boundaries: one accumulator serves the whole transmission; a symbol change alters only the slope. Phase jumps would splatter sidebands and rob the decoder of the clean tone lines it correlates against.

Contrast: MSK

MSK is CPFSK with h = 0.5 (binary). WSPR is its 4-ary, slower cousin at h = 1 — same family, different constants, all cited in docs/protocol.md.

Part 3 · Communication theory

The decibel, worked until it is boring

Every link budget is six multiplications disguised as additions. Do this one slowly once, and never fear dB again:

# thermal noise power in a bandwidth B, at 290 K
N_dBm = -174 + 10*log10(B_Hz)

# B = 2500 Hz (the WSPR reference bandwidth)
10*log10(2500)   = 33.98 dB
N_dBm            = -174.0 + 33.98 = -140.0 dBm

# WSPR acceptance: signal 31 dB BELOW that noise
P_signal         = -140.0 - 31    = -171.0 dBm
                 = 10^((-171-30)/10) W
                 = 8 × 10⁻²¹ W

# noise DENSITY (per 1 Hz): the reference for C/N0
N0               = -174.0 dBm/Hz
C/N0             = P_signal - N0  = +3.0 dB·Hz
Why dBm/Hz exists Noise power scales with bandwidth; dividing it out gives a property of the receiver input alone. C/N₀ says “how much carrier per hertz of noise” — bandwidth-free, hence the natural currency before you choose a detection bandwidth (SNR, dBm).

Part 3 · Communication theory

The matched filter and why FEC completes the story

Correlation is the receiver

The optimal detector for a known signal in white noise is the matched filter: multiply by the expected waveform and integrate. wsprd does exactly this twice over — first to find the sync pattern (part 4), then per symbol to pick the best tone. The 54 dB “gain” above is nothing mystical: it is the matched filter's integration.

Why FEC lets you live at Eb/N₀ ≈ 6 dB

Raw 4-FSK at h = 1 needs ≈ 9–10 dB Eb/N₀ for usable symbol errors. WSPR operates 3 dB lower because a rate-½, K=32 convolutional code (part 4) plus sequential decoding converts a sparse error pattern into zero errors — forward error correction trades bandwidth (×2 redundancy + sync) for a lower operating point.

Book Sklar, Digital Communications, ch. 5–7 (FSK, detection, FEC) — the clearest treatment of the detection triangle in print. Proakis & Salehi ch. 4/7 for the rigorous version.
Part 3 · Communication theory

The matched filter, by hand

Strip away the jargon: the matched filter asks, for each candidate tone, “how much of this exact wave is in my noisy recording?” — multiply and add.

Why integrate instead of average?

Same thing, different scaling — the point is coherence: signal samples add with their sign intact (amplitude × T), noise adds in root-T (σ × √T). The ratio grows as √T — twice the integration, 3 dB better. WSPR integrates 110.6 s; that is the whole magic of part 3, restated.

Book Sklar ch. 7 derives the matched filter from maximum likelihood; Lyons ch. 11 gives the most intuitive pictures of correlation in print.

Part 4 · The WSPR protocol

WSPR: a protocol engineered for −31 dB

WSPR (Weak Signal Propagation Reporting, pronounced “whisper”) was written by Joe Taylor, K1JT (Nobel laureate physicist) and released in 2008 as part of the WSJT family. It is a measurement protocol: beacons transmit, receivers report what they heard to wsprnet.org, and the collective map reveals propagation.

The frame

  • Transmissions start 1.000 s into an even UTC minute (spec §7 — frozen by the empirical adjudication of part 8).
  • Duration 110.592 s, then silence to the next even minute.
  • 162 channel symbols × 0.682667 s; four tones 1.46484375 Hz apart.
  • Band segments per band plan (part 7); slot masks reduce duty cycle.

Part 4 · The WSPR protocol

Type-1 message: 50 bits is enough

A WSPR message is a callsign, a 4-character Maidenhead grid, and a power in dBm — e.g. “K1ABC FN42 37” (37 dBm = 5 W). All of it fits in 50 bits:

Mixed radix, the idea Positional restrictions are free compression: a callsign's first char is a letter (26 values), char 2 is alphanumeric (36), char 3 a digit (10), chars 4–6 letters/space (27 each). 26·36·10·27³ ≈ 1.8 × 10⁸ < 2²⁸ ≈ 2.7 × 10⁸ — it fits. The same trick packs the grid (A–R letters, digits) into 15 bits. Type-2 (prefix/suffix) and Type-3 (<hash>) messages exist in the protocol but are out of scope for this beacon (the model rejects them with a clear error).

Part 4 · The WSPR protocol

FEC: a K=32 convolutional code

WSPR protects the 50 payload bits with a rate-½ convolutional code of constraint length K = 32 — extraordinary by terrestrial standards:

  • 50 payload + 31 flush zeros = 81 input bits → 162 coded bits.
  • Polynomials POLY1 = F2D05351, POLY2 = E4613C47 (Layland–Lushbaugh), 32-bit shift register, newest bit in the LSB; POLY1 parity emitted first (protocol.md §3).
  • The 31 tail zeros flush the register so the trellis ends in a known state.
Why K=32, and why not Viterbi? A long constraint length buys a large free distance — more errors corrected per bit of redundancy — which is exactly what a −31 dB link needs. The catch: Viterbi decoding needs 2³¹ states, which is not a thing. WSPR instead uses sequential decoding (Fano / stack algorithms — sequential decoding) plus optional ordered-statistics decoding (OSD), trading worst-case effort for typical-case success. Hardware echo: our RTL only encodes — a 32-bit register and two XOR trees (wspr_fec.vhd).

The tail, and why 31 zeros matter

The shift register must end where the decoder knows it ends — otherwise the decoder must hypothesize 2³² ending states. K = 32 ⇒ 31 extra input bits (the first input bit occupies the register at once) flush every payload bit out the far end. The tail costs 62 coded bits (38% of the frame!) and buys a decodable end state — the rate is honestly 50/162, and the budget of part 3 used exactly that.

Trellis in one breath A convolutional code is a walk on a graph whose nodes are register states; each input bit moves you along a labelled edge (the two parity bits). Decoding = finding the most likely walk. K = 32 ⇒ 2³¹ nodes — hence sequential decoding: walk greedily, backtrack when the metric collapses (the Fano algorithm's “push your luck, then retreat” rule).
Part 4 · The WSPR protocol

Interleaving and the sync comb

Interleave: scatter the errors

Sequential decoding fails when errors cluster. WSPR permutes the 162 coded bits with a fixed bit-reversal-with-carry permutation — frozen, not “an equivalent” one (wspr_interleave.vhd). Unlike plain bit-reversal it is not an involution (verified numerically with the golden model): encoder and decoder both apply the same forward table.

Sync: 63 of 162

A fixed 162-bit sync vector (from WSPRcode.f90:17-26) marks 63 of the 162 symbols. Each channel symbol is tone = 2·data + sync: when the sync bit is set, the symbol jumps to the odd tone regardless of data — a known pattern the decoder can correlate against.

Folklore check “The sync vector has 30 ones” floats around the internet. The golden vector — wsprcode-verified — has 63. When memory and the pinned source disagree, the pinned source wins (this is rule 2 of the repo, and part 8's whole theme).
Part 4 · The WSPR protocol

Sync in action: what the decoder does with 63 marks

The decoder slides a 162-symbol template over the received symbol stream — over time (≈ ±5.4 s around the slot start) and frequency (±110 Hz) — and scores each offset by how well the 63 marked positions land on odd tones:

  • Right time, right frequency: 63 of 63 sync symbols hit → enormous correlation peak (they add coherently; the data symbols average out).
  • Wrong by one symbol: many marks miss → the score collapses. The comb is deliberately non-periodic, so no wrong offset pretends to fit.
  • Partial overlap (TX started near the file edge): the decoder tolerates missing symbols by scoring them as soft “no opinion” values — wsprd.c's “partial decoding”, documented at the sync-search comment.

Then 162 one-symbol decisions

With the offset locked, each symbol's 0.6827 s of samples are reduced to four numbers (energy per tone — the four correlators of part 3), mapped to a soft symbol value 0…3 plus a confidence. That stream feeds the sequential decoder, which reconstructs the 50-bit payload and — if the tail state and checksum agree — the message. The whole decode is two passes: decode, subtract, decode again.

Inside wsprd: the decoder in five moves

GPS: buying the truth for $12

Two products of one module

  • 1PPS — a pulse per second, disciplined to the atomic clocks behind GPS: the time reference (pulse per second).

The checksum, honestly computed

XOR every ASCII byte of GPRMC,123519,A (delimiters excluded):

G ^ P ^ R ^ M ^ C ^ , ^ 1 ^ 2 ^
3 ^ 5 ^ 1 ^ 9 ^ , ^ A = 0x07

The parser folds each incoming byte into an 8-bit accumulator (checksum <= checksum xor data(6 downto 0) — bit 7 is dropped, ASCII keeps it zero) and compares against the two hex digits after *. Commit happens only at the CRLF — a sentence is a unit or it is nothing.

  • A corrupted byte anywhere invalidates the whole sentence — but the already committed time stays untouched (plan §31: corrupt NMEA never changes valid time).

  • Review S8: a stream without CRLF swallows the next sentence's $ — the commit state now re-arms on $. Every fix traces to a finding.

Part 5 · The beacon architecture

Measuring your own clock against the GPS second

pps_measure.vhd counts clock cycles between 1PPS rising edges. Two details make it correct:

  • Reset-to-1: the interval counter re-arms to 1, so the latched value is exactly the cycles in the completed second (the classic off-by-one, explained in the header — the model teaching artifact of this repo).
  • Timeout: no edge for 24 000 000 cycles (two GPS seconds) ⇒ measurement_valid='0' — PPS loss propagates (plan §31).

Averaging: √N, not N (review S1)

Each measurement carries ±1 cycle of quantization. Averaging CALIBRATION_SECONDS = 8 valid measurements reduces the standard error ≈ √N for dithered jitter — but the worst-case bias stays below 1 cycle/s (≈ 0.083 ppm) no matter how long you average. The header says exactly this after the review; the earlier “divides the uncertainty by N” was wrong.

Then: round-half-up division, bounds check ±1000 ppm (11 988 000…12 012 000), out-of-bounds ⇒ calibration_error + calibration_valid='0' ⇒ no transmit (plan §31). The value freezes for the whole transmission and is re-measured before the next one (plan §7).

Calibration, worked with numbers

Suppose the real oscillator is 12 000 012 Hz (a plausible crystal) and quantization dithers each 1PPS count by ±1. Eight seconds of measurement might read:

11 999 998   12 000 012   12 000 013   12 000 011   12 000 012   11 999 999   12 000 012   12 000 014
  • Average = 96 000 071/8 = 12 000 008.875 → round-half-up ⇒ 12 000 009 Hz.
  • Error vs nominal: +9/12 000 000 = +0.75 ppm — inside the ±1000 ppm bounds (11 988 000…12 012 000), so calibration_valid='1'.
  • Effect on the RF carrier: +0.75 ppm × 10 140 200 Hz = +7.6 Hz of dial error — the decoder's ±110 Hz search eats it easily. Effect on the tone spacing: +0.75 ppm × 1.4648 Hz = +1.1 µHz — nothing. Absolute error searches; relative error destroys. The clock error is almost all absolute.

Then freeze it

The averaged value is frozen (calibration_frozen) for the whole transmission — recalibrating mid-TX would shift tones mid-symbol. After TX_DONE, the FSM returns to CALIBRATE: a fresh measurement precedes every transmission (plan §7).

Out of bounds? If the average misses the bounds (say a 12.5 MHz clock), calibration_error and calibration_frozen assert together, valid stays low, the scheduler re-pulses cal_start, and nothing transmits. The M5 LED bug lived exactly in this corner.

Part 6 · The modulator

The 130 ns multiply — thinking like synthesis

A first-draft modulator wrote phase_increment = CARRIER + TONE_INCREMENT * symbol — one line of VHDL, and a timing bomb:

  • TONE_INCREMENT is 40 bits; symbol is 2 bits. A combinational 40×2 multiply is a chain of partial-product additions: ≈ 130 ns on this fabric — while the clock period is 83.3 ns. The design would not close timing at any clock above ~7.7 MHz.
  • The fix that costs nothing: the symbol takes only four values, so precompute the four sums at build time and let a 4-way multiplexer pick one: INC_T0..INC_T3. Multiplication collapses into selection — ~2 ns through one LUT level plus routing.
  • The build-time constants come from tools/calculate_nco.py, with the +1 LSB rounding note for HALF_TONE (2·67109 = 134218 vs 134217).
The general lesson On an FPGA, expression and cost are different things. A one-line arithmetic statement synthesizes into real gates with real propagation delay. The craft is spotting which computations are (a) constant-folding candidates, (b) worth pipelining, (c) worth replacing with a table. Here: (a), obviously, in hindsight — and in foresight once you have seen one multiply ruin a timing report.

Part 6 · The modulator

Symbol timing: the Bresenham trick

The symbol duration is CLOCK_HZ · 8192 / 12000 cycles — not an integer for a real (calibrated) clock. The modulator splits it exactly: WHOLE + REM/12000, and distributes the remainder with a Bresenham error term:

symbol k gets WHOLE+1 cycles iff ((k+1)·REM mod 12000) ≥ 12000 − REM ⇒ cumulative length of N symbols is EXACTLY ⌊N·CLOCK_HZ·8192/12000⌋

Worked example (CLOCK_HZ = 12 001): WHOLE = 8192, REM = 8192, so lengths alternate 8192/8193 and the 162-symbol total is exact. At the nominal 12 MHz, REM = 0 and every symbol is exactly 8192 cycles.

Why it matters A decoder measures tone timing across all 162 symbols. A rounding error of even one cycle per symbol would drift the grid by ~1.4 ms over a transmission — visible to the sync search. Zero cumulative error is the whole point (plan §10).

Part 6 · The modulator

Regression armor, dissected: the M7 tone check

The integration test that would have caught M1 works by making tone classes countable. With the scaled NCO (10-bit accumulator, CARRIER = 256, HALF = 64), tone k uses increment 64 + 128·k, and one 8192-cycle symbol advances the phase by exactly 8192·inc/1024 = 8·inc whole wraps — an integer! Therefore:

rising edges per symbol = 8 · inc(k) = 512 + 1024·k ⇒ 512, 1536, 2560, 3584 — four classes, thousands apart
  • The testbench counts rising edges per symbol window and compares against TONE_GOLDEN(k) — all 162 of them (±2 for the anchored first and gated last window).
  • A one-symbol skew compares symbol k against golden k−1. Since consecutive golden tones differ by ≥ 1024 edges, any skew fails loudly.
  • The total transition count is also checked exactly: 679 935 measured vs 679 936 expected.

Part 7 · The RF chain

Filter design in one slide, then one evening

The theory you actually need

  • A filter is a frequency-selective voltage divider built from resonators. A series LC is a short at resonance; a parallel LC is an open. Combine them and you steer frequencies (band-pass filter).
  • Loaded Q = f₀/BW sets the sharpness: high Q ⇒ narrow and lossy, low Q ⇒ sloppy skirts.
  • Butterworth = flattest passband; Chebyshev = steeper skirts for passband ripple. For WSPR, 2–4 poles of Butterworth is plenty.
  • Resonator: f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)) — pick L, solve C.
Worked exercise (not a ratified design) Design a 2-pole Butterworth BPF: f₀ = 3.5701 MHz, BW = 100 kHz ⇒ Qloaded = 35.7. Choose L = 1 µH ⇒ C = 1/((2πf₀)²L) ≈ 1.99 nF. Verify in a circuit simulator, then measure — docs/rf.md §8 requires the measured pin spectrum before final values are chosen. Classic build literature: the ARRL Handbook; Hayward et al., Experimental Methods in RF Design (“EMRFD”).

A 2-pole band-pass, fully worked

Target: pass 3 570 100 Hz (80 m) with ~100 kHz of bandwidth; reject the 12 MHz clock line and the 3f harmonic at 10.71 MHz. Topology: two resonators — series LC (short at resonance) then shunt LC (open at resonance):

Q = f0 / BW = 3 570 100 / 100 000 = 35.7

# 2. Choose L = 1 µH (a chunky, low-loss coil),
#    solve the resonator for C:
C = 1 / (2π·f0)² · L
  = 1 / ((2π·3.5701e6)² · 1e-6) = 1.99 nF

#    (impedance-invert the ladder; ARRL/EMRFD tables)
# 4. Estimate insertion loss ~ 1–2 dB for Q_L 35

Check the rejection at 12 MHz: the response of a 2-pole Butterworth falls as (f/f₀)−2 beyond the band — 12/3.57 = 3.36 ⇒ ≈ −10.5 dB… not enough alone for the clock line. Add a notch or a third pole. This is why the procedure ends in measurement (docs/rf.md §8), not in a parts list.

Levels: honest arithmetic

  • A 0–3.3 V square wave's fundamental is 4·(3.3/2)/π ≈ 2.10 Vpk (1.48 V RMS) ⇒ +16.4 dBm into 50 Ω — but the pin cannot deliver it.
  • 3.3 V into 50 Ω is ~66 mA — far beyond a logic pin. Keep the load high-impedance: series R or a buffer stage. The iCE40 data-sheet limit is PENDING-HARDWARE (docs/rf.md §4).
  • Through a 330 Ω series resistor: ≈ 0.20 V RMS into 50 Ω ≈ −1 dBm. WSPR decodes to −31 dB SNR — milliwatts get spotted on 160/80 m with a real antenna.
  • The 30 m image starts ~15 dB down (part 2) — subtract that from any image-band budget.
Ghost images, one last look The pin's spectrum is a comb of images m·fs ± f — the sampled-data “ghost” of part 1, now at RF. The superhet image frequency and the TV ghost are the same disease in other organs: once you can see images, you find them in every receiver block diagram you will ever read.
Repo docs/rf.md is the full annex: band table with increments, the tone-order rule and its numeric proof, filter checklist, level estimates, legal rules, PENDING-HARDWARE list.
Part 7 · Recap & pitfalls

Part 7 distilled

You can now…

  • Draw the full transmit spectrum for any band from the m·fs ± f rule and the square wave's odd harmonics.
  • Run the band-pass design arithmetic (Q, resonator C, rejection estimate) and know when the estimate says “add a pole or a notch”.
  • Compute pin levels honestly and explain why −1 dBm from a resistor is still a workable WSPR beacon.

Three ways to kill a decode from the analog side

  • Filter the wrong line — the fold is stronger but mirrored; a 1.86 MHz “30 m beacon” decodes as nothing at all (part 2's rule).
  • Let the clock line through — 12 MHz is unmodulated and strong; a lazy filter puts a carrier grave jacket over your noise floor... actually worse: it lands in other services' receivers and invites the regulator to yours.
  • Over-spend the budget — 6 dB of avoidable insertion loss on the 30 m image is most of the image's headroom. Count dB like euros.
Check yourself A friend proposes transmitting on 20 m (14 097 100 Hz). Using the docs/rf.md §2 table: what is the usable line's sinc weight, and which line would a lazy filter grab? Testbench patterns that matter

The delta-cycle trap

# Registered outputs read at the SAME clock edge
# show the PREVIOUS value (delta-0 semantics):
wait until rising_edge(clk);
if rf_out = '1' then ...  # this is the OLD sample

# Sample one edge later, or use the event:
wait until rising_edge(clk);
wait until rising_edge(clk);

This caused several false testbench failures during development. It is VHDL's delta-cycle model, not a bug — and knowing it is the difference between a TB that lies and one that tests.

Scale parameters, never logic

Simulating 110.6 s at 12 MHz is 1.3 billion cycles. So the design makes everything a generic (plan §21): the integration TB runs CLOCK_HZ = 12000, BAUD = 100, a 10-bit NCO — the functional logic is identical, only the numbers scale. The tone-identity check works precisely because the scaled NCO makes each tone's edge count distinct (512·(1+2k) per symbol).

The negative paths are the product Missing PPS, out-of-bounds calibration, corrupt NMEA, reset during TX — each failure mode of plan §31 has a testbench scenario. Untested failure handling is folklore.
Practice 8.1 make sim TB=tb_wspr_top reads ~5 minutes of scaled time. Find its seven checks in the header, then add an eighth: after the TX, verify led2 returns ready (it is already there — check 7). Read the M7 tone monitor process and explain the anchor choice.

Part 9 · VHDL & FPGA primer

VHDL-93 in one slide

library ieee; use ieee.std_logic_1164.all; use ieee.numeric_std.all;

entity nco is                       -- the interface (a “socket”)
  generic ( ACCUMULATOR_BITS : positive := 40 );  -- build-time knobs
  port ( clk, rst : in std_logic;
         increment : in std_logic_vector(ACCUMULATOR_BITS-1 downto 0);
         rf_out    : out std_logic );
end entity nco;

architecture rtl of nco is
  signal phase_acc : unsigned(ACCUMULATOR_BITS-1 downto 0) := (others => '0');
begin
  p : process (clk)                -- runs when clk changes; flip-flops inferred
  begin
    if rising_edge(clk) then
      if rst = '1' then phase_acc <= (others => '0');   -- synchronous reset
      else phase_acc <= phase_acc + unsigned(increment);
      end if;
    end if;
  end process p;
  rf_out <= phase_acc(ACCUMULATOR_BITS-1);   -- concurrent: pure wiring
end architecture rtl;
The three habits this repo demands (1) Synchronous resets, one clock domain. (2) Named constants with provenance — no bare literals (plan §6). (3) --std=93 on every invocation; the strict sweep adds -fexplicit -Wbinding and greps for VHDL-2008 constructs (process(all), std.env, protected types) that must not appear. Book: Ashenden, The Designer's Guide to VHDL; Pong Chu, FPGA Prototyping by VHDL Examples for the hands-on style.

Where the 40-bit adder went

Synthesis maps arithmetic onto FPGA fabric: each carry-chain slice computes a few sum bits per LUT. The iCE40UP5K has 5280 LUTs; our design uses a few thousand — the accumulator, the FSMs, the UART shift registers. No block-RAM, no DSP multipliers (we removed the one that mattered in part 6).

icetime then walks the routed netlist and reports the slowest clock-to-clock path: our 32.16 ns vs the 83.3 ns requirement — a 2.6× margin, which is what “works on the desk” means in numbers.

Timing closure in one sentence Every signal must arrive at its destination flip-flop before the next clock edge. Delay = logic levels + routing. Margins are measured, not assumed — that is why make build ends with a timing report, not a success slogan.
